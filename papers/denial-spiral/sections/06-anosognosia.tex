\section{Why the crisis resists diagnosis}
\label{sec:anosognosia}
%% ==========================================================================

\begin{remark}[Obscurity anosognosia --- why the crisis resists diagnosis]
\label{rem:anosognosia}
Four censoring mechanisms compound. Three remove the evidence that would settle
whether the Lemma's prediction is being enacted in the field; the fourth removes the
response to the evidence, and so survives its publication.

The first is the one \S\ref{sec:obscurity} has already named: obscurity does not advertise
itself. No vendor documents a scheme as depending on $\Attacker$'s ignorance, so
for any given victim the question \emph{was this holder running an
obscurity-dependent design?} has no recorded answer. The exposure variable is not
merely unmeasured but unmeasurable from public sources, which forecloses the
obvious epidemiological question --- what do the victims of prolonged coercion, or
of the terminal move, have in common?

The second is recorded in \S\ref{sec:registry}: a homicide is reported as a
homicide, not as a robbery whose victim was then killed, so the enactments most
diagnostic of the Lemma are the least likely to enter the registry at all.

The third forecloses the one route by which the first two might have been worked
around. Where $\Victim$ is killed, the people who survive them are the only
remaining source for what that holder was running --- and they are, by
construction, the party least able to supply it. A scheme that depended on
$\Attacker$'s ignorance depended on $\Victim$'s discretion, and discretion is not
selective: the family was not told either. Where they do know, disclosure carries
no benefit to them and an evident cost --- it advertises that a household which
held bitcoin may hold it still --- so it is withheld unless it might help resolve
the case. The one person who could have answered the question is the one
Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} predicts will not survive to be asked.

The fourth is of a different kind, and is why publishing the first three does not
dissolve them. Conceding the argument is expensive to whoever concedes. A vendor who
grants that a shipped feature is worse than its own absence has said something about
products already in customers' hands; a trainer who grants that coached denial depletes
the credibility of all denials has said it about a service already sold; a holder who
grants it has said it about a setup they are still running. The cost of conceding is
private, immediate and borne by the conceder; the cost of \emph{not} conceding is
diffuse, deferred and borne by holders who are not in the room. That is the same
commons structure as the Denial Spiral (Remark~\ref{rem:denial-spiral}), one level up:
there the depleted resource is the credibility of denials, here it is the field's
capacity to correct itself, and in both the individually rational move is to wait for
someone else to go first. Recalcitrance is cheapest exactly where it is costliest in
aggregate.

The precedent usually reached for is Semmelweis, and it repays being stated rather than
gestured at. In 1847, after his colleague Jakob Kolletschka died of a wound taken from a
scalpel during an autopsy and showed the pathology of childbed fever, Semmelweis
inferred that the fever was carried on the hands of physicians arriving from the
dissecting room, and imposed washing in chlorinated lime; mortality in his clinic fell
from roughly $18\%$ to roughly $2\%$ within the year~\cite{semmelweis1861}. The
inference was available two decades before the germ theory that would explain it, and it
was rejected anyway --- not on evidential grounds alone but on positional ones, since
accepting it meant accepting that physicians' own hands had carried the
disease~\cite{carter-childbed}. That is the narrow aptness, and it is the same shape as
the mechanism above.

The wider instruction cuts against us, and we take it. When the 1861 monograph failed to
persuade, Semmelweis turned to open letters denouncing his critics as murderers. The
denunciations did not shorten the interval before adoption; Pasteur's germ theory and
Lister's antisepsis did, two decades on, and the interval was measured in
lives~\cite{carter-childbed}. It is one reason this paper states a bar rather than an
indictment, classifies designs rather than people, and credits vendors where the record
credits them (\S\ref{sec:obscurity}).

That restraint is also what keeps this remark from becoming unfalsifiable. A mechanism
that predicts silence will be read as agreement is a license, not a mechanism, and we
decline it: the claim is that concession is \emph{costly}, not that any particular
non-response is \emph{concealed assent}. Silence has ordinary causes --- time,
authority, counsel, disagreement never written down --- and nothing here licenses an
inference about any individual who did not reply. What it predicts is aggregate: that
the availability of the analysis will not by itself produce its adoption, and that
adoption arrives first wherever conceding is made cheap --- a neutral weakness class to
cite, a reviewed venue to cite it from, a bar defined before anyone is measured against
it.

So the covariate is censored by what vendors do not say, the numerator by how the
terminal event is classified, what remains by what the bereaved do not know or will not
repeat, and the correction by what it costs the corrector. The structure is that of \emph{anosognosia} --- the dementia patient whose disease
erodes memory and, with it, the capacity to notice that memory is eroding. One
cause, two effects, and the second conceals the first. Obscurity is simultaneously
what makes a design fail under Kerckhoffs and what erases the record by which the
failure would be recognized. We draw no empirical claim from this: we cannot,
which is the point. What it establishes is that the diagnosis is not available
from the data by waiting, and that a field which waits for it will keep not
finding what it is not positioned to see. That is an argument for the formal
route --- the bar has to be derived, because it will not be measured.
\end{remark}
