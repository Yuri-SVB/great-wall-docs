\section{Classification of existing designs}
\label{sec:classify}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Corollary~\ref{cor:reach} gives a classification axis: a design that clears
Criterion~\ref{prin:nga} does so either by \emph{removing the race} or by
\emph{removing $\Attacker$'s reach over the racer}. The framework should
classify shipping designs, and it does --- via the reachability corollary, not
by calling them broken.

\subsection{Time-lock-rescue vaults}

Take \textbf{Rewind Bitcoin}~\cite{rewind-bitcoin} as the exemplar. Funds sit in
a Bitcoin-native time-lock vault; unlocking starts a multi-day countdown; if an
unauthorized unlock is triggered, the owner \emph{or a trusted delegate} has that
window to press ``Rescue'' and sweep to a cold panic address.

Against \emph{remote} theft it is sound: a thief who steals keys trips the
countdown, the owner notices, and rescues. But the spend capability sits behind
\emph{only a delay}, with keys on the seized device and no recall floor --- so a
wrench attacker who seizes the device \emph{does} hold a feasible path (unlock,
wait out the countdown). That is a race, in the sense of Lemma~\ref{lem:drl}.
Rewind's answer is exactly the second route of Corollary~\ref{cor:reach}: it puts
the winning racer out of $\Attacker$'s reach --- a \emph{remote delegate} the
attacker cannot find or eliminate presses Rescue --- so the attack fails
cleanly. This is a legitimate clearance, but the remote-racer kind: its cost is
that \textbf{custody is no longer individual}.

\begin{remark}[When the escape closes]\label{rem:escape-closes}
The remedy holds only while the delegate is genuinely remote. If the delegate is
a friend who happens to be visiting during the attack, \emph{both} problems
apply at once --- custody was already non-individual, and the racer is now
co-located and reachable --- so the design becomes deadly-race-susceptible
again. The user is thus forced into an awkward corner: trust someone they can
rarely be near, and accept a perverse incentive to avoid physical proximity to a
trusted friend.
\end{remark}

\subsection{A second time-lock design --- BitVault}

\textbf{BitVault}~\cite{bitvault} is the same species. No authoritative
specification is published: the vendor describes the product as ``100\% Open
Source,'' but its code link resolves to an organization with no public
repositories (archived at \url{http://archive.today/2026.09.01-225418/https://github.com/BitVaulty}), and its documentation
and audit are served through a gated document-sharing service. What we have is the
vendor's pages, a concept whitepaper~\cite{bssl} that is self-attributed and hosted
outside the vendor's own links, and correspondence of 26--27 August 2026 (all
accessed 1 September 2026); they do not agree in every particular. This bounds what
can be claimed and is \emph{not} an allegation: we have no evidence that any
material was withdrawn, and observed none at any earlier time.\footnote{The
architectural description is the company's; the classification is the present
author's, and is relative to this paper's threat model. In correspondence the
company confirmed that the current configuration employs no autonomous,
un-coercible watchtower and no pre-signed rescue sweep; that rejection of a pending
authorization requires intervention by the user, a separately positioned
user-controlled device or signer, or a designated party; and that once the required
signatures and timelocks are satisfied the resulting transaction cannot be
reversed. It further agreed that the ``publicly verifiable, making kidnapping
attempts pointless'' formulation was too categorical, and describes the decoy layer
as plausible deniability rather than a guarantee.}

Taking that whitepaper as valid, the spending paths carry timelocks: a primary
needing a custodian co-signature behind a configurable
$2\,\mathrm{h}$--$15\,\mathrm{d}$ relative CSV, a one-year fallback on the user's
own two keys, and a three-year custodian path, the last two with no gatekeeper.
The policy gates the primary on \texttt{older()} and both fallbacks on
\texttt{after()}, so the primary delay is a maturation from funding rather than a
window on a request, and the multi-year locks do not re-arm. Both cases fail.
Before the one-year date $\Attacker$ coerces $A$ and $B$ and kills, the only party
who would tell the gatekeeper to withhold being $\Victim$: removal is what buys
settlement, which is Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} exactly, and the pivotality is the
victim's as \emph{witness} rather than as racer, since the primary path needs the
custodian's signature and $\Victim$ cannot use it alone either. After the date he
coerces $A$ and $B$ and spends at once, ungatekept --- no delay, no notification,
nothing to cancel.

Property~\ref{p:know} we leave open: the documentation assumes hardware and offers
an optional ``copy of $A$ kept in a separate location,'' but nothing in the
construction forbids running it from memorized seeds and re-deriving on demand,
which would be deviceless. Property~\ref{p:indiv} fails in both directions --- the
optional off-chain gatekeeper can deny a user the quick path by declining to
release the custodian's co-signature, and past the three-year lock a custodian and
that gatekeeper can spend together.

Property~\ref{p:nonob} we also leave open, for a reason worth stating generally:
\textbf{obscurity does not advertise itself}, so classifying a design as
obscurity-dependent is always an inference from what it would have to assume in
order to make sense. Here the deadly-race exposure is plain on the published
policy, plain enough that a Kerckhoffs-aware design would not ship it; the design
therefore \emph{suggests} an $\Attacker$ assumed ignorant of it --- a reading the
scarcity of public-facing documentation would fit. The only other
reading we can construct is an $\Attacker$ assumed willing to coerce but not to
kill --- not a milder premise but a wager on his $c$
(Definition~\ref{def:priced}), staked on the holder's life. Undetermined is the
honest entry between the two, and it costs nothing: the coercion result stands on
structure either way.

A further caveat sharpens \ref{r:remotewinner} itself, and a distinction sharpens
the caveat. What matters is not only whether the delegate is remote, but what
$\Attacker$ needs from them. Where the delegate's required contribution is an
\emph{act} --- a payment, a signature --- coercion routes through $\Victim$ to
them and a humane delegate performs it. The act is self-verifying: $\Attacker$
knows when he has succeeded. The Balland case~\cite[\S7]{denial-spiral} is exactly
this failure, and it is the reality clause~\ref{d:nodelegatecoerce} idealizes
away.

Where the required contribution is an \emph{omission}, the analysis does not
transfer. To win, $\Attacker$ needs the delegate to abstain for the length of the
window, and abstention has no verifiable permanence: the delegate may comply
visibly while retaining the means invisibly, and by NNPP
(Assumption~\ref{lem:nnpl}) cannot be made to demonstrate that no retained means
exists --- an undisclosed duplicate of a cancellation credential being, among the
available evasions, close to the cheapest. The same negative existential that
convicts $\Victim$ in the individual case, that one cannot prove one has nothing
left to give, acquits the delegate here, because it is now $\Attacker$ who needs
the proof and cannot obtain it.

The incentive inverts with it. In the individual configuration, removing $\Victim$
\emph{wins} the race: the only party who could cancel is gone. Where the pivotal
racer is unreached and may hold an undisclosed means of canceling, removing
$\Victim$ \emph{loses} it --- the threat that keeps the delegate passive expires
with the hostage, and the delegate then acts. $\Attacker$'s dominant strategy
turns from removal to preservation, and he comes to need $\Victim$ not merely
alive but demonstrably so. This is the mechanical content of
\ref{r:remotewinner}: not simply that the pivotal racer is out of reach, but that
putting them out of reach makes $\Victim$'s survival instrumental to $\Attacker$
rather than an obstacle to him.

The exchange should be recorded as an exchange. NNPP protects the funds by
denying $\Attacker$ satisfaction, and it does so by relocating the pressure: an
$\Attacker$ who cannot verify the delegate's incapacity has reason to press the
delegate. Shared custody does not dissolve the problem so much as move it to a
party who is harder to reach and less exposed. That is a defensible trade, and it
is why \ref{r:remotewinner} is an honest route rather than a solved one.

\subsection{The industry recommendation set}
\label{sub:industry}

The two designs above are products; the same structure appears in the
\emph{standard advice}. Industry threat-intelligence reporting on wrench attacks
states the objective in terms close to ours --- custody should ``remain resilient
even when a human is under duress'' --- and recommends, among other controls,
\textbf{withdrawal delays}, transaction-size thresholds, \textbf{staged vault
architecture}, \textbf{emergency freeze} processes, and multisig or MPC with
\textbf{geographically distributed signers}~\cite{certik-intel3d-h1-2026}.

Criterion~\ref{prin:nga} does not reject this advice; it \emph{partitions} it, and
that partition is the framework's practical content. Each of these controls
interposes a delay or a veto, so each defines a competing racer, and the question
Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} forces is only whether $\Attacker$ can reach whoever holds the
release:
\begin{itemize}[nosep,leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Where the freeze or cancellation authority rests with a \emph{remote,
        institutionally separated} party --- a provider desk, an autonomous
        watchtower, a co-signer beyond reach --- the control clears the bar by
        route~\ref{r:remotewinner}, at that route's standing cost: custody is no
        longer individual.
  \item Where the delay is one the \emph{holder} can release, or the freeze
        authority is a device or a person on the scene, the control leaves
        $\Attacker$ a feasible-but-unfinished path and is therefore a race --- the
        gray outcome the Criterion forbids, and the configuration in which the
        control is worse than its absence.
\end{itemize}
Geographic distribution of signers is the same statement made in space rather
than in time (Definition~\ref{def:geographic}), and inherits the same condition.
The recommendations are thus sound \emph{exactly when} the reachability question
has been asked and answered --- and it is that question, rather than the length of
the delay, that separates a delay which buys rescue from a delay which buys
$\Attacker$ a reason to eliminate the other racer.

\subsection{Self-destruct (wallet-erase) PINs, and the decoy beside them}
\label{sub:selfdestruct}

The most widely shipped consumer countermeasure is a PIN that wipes the device,
often paired with a decoy wallet reached by a second passphrase. The companion
paper~\cite{denial-spiral} classifies the class in full, against the vendors'
own documentation; three of its findings are needed here.

\emph{The class occupies the naive tier} (Definition~\ref{def:naive-cr}): each
mechanism works only against an attacker ignorant of a documented feature, and
the same documentation that sells it teaches him what to ask for. \emph{The
erasure presupposes what defeats it}: the vendor documentation assumes a
surviving backup, so wiping the device removes the device path while leaving
intact the path that runs through the holder --- which is the path coercion
uses. \emph{And the counsel is self-defeating in aggregate}: a norm of coached
denial depletes the credibility of all denials, so an interrogation opening with
one earns further coercion rather than release. That is the \textbf{Denial
Spiral}, the companion's title result, and it is a distinct mechanism from the
Deadly Race --- it prices the \emph{duration} of an encounter where the Race
prices the \emph{terminal} move.

The class matters to this paper for one structural reason. It fails on
non-obscurity (Property~\ref{p:nonob}) and \emph{not} on the Criterion: a
self-destruct destroys a path and names no second racer, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} does not reach it. Its rows in \S\ref{sub:tgpo-comparison}
are DR-safe and condemned anyway, which is what the two columns are there to
separate.
\subsection{TGPO, and the honest comparison}
\label{sub:tgpo-comparison}

TGPO clears by the \emph{other} route: the secret is tacit and never on
the device (Assumption~\ref{ass:tkba}, Theorem~\ref{thm:floor}), so a seizure
yields nothing feasible --- there is no racer to reach, and the time-lock is
only a delay \emph{on top of} an already-clean failure. Read through the Four
Properties (Corollary~\ref{cor:fourprops}), the comparison is a covering-set
statement: each shipping design buys (or tries to buy) coercion resistance by 
\emph{trading at least one} property TGPO keeps --- and one of them trades a
property and \emph{still} fails. TGPO is the only design holding all four.

\noindent Column~(\ref{p:know}) is the covering point: TGPO is the \emph{only} knowledge-based
(deviceless --- the secret is knowledge, never a device or a location) row, because
every competitor keeps a \emph{transmissible} secret on a device. The fifth property
of \S\ref{sub:hostage} adds no column: by Corollary~\ref{cor:hostage-equiv} it is
\emph{equivalent} to that one, so it would reproduce it cell for cell. Neither does the
Denial Spiral, which is equivalent to Column~(\ref{p:nonob}) rather than to that
one~\cite[Cor.~2]{denial-spiral}.

\emph{The \textbf{DR-safe} column is not derivable from the other four}, and that is why
it is there. It asks the criterion directly --- \emph{can} a single encounter leave a state of
positive pivotality? --- and the answer does not track the properties: \textbf{Rewind}
forfeits two of them and is DR-safe nonetheless, clearing by \ref{r:remotewinner}, while
\textbf{BitVault} forfeits them and is not. The \textbf{self-destruct PIN} is the
instructive row: it is DR-safe and still condemned, because a self-destruct destroys a
path and names no second racer, so Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} does not reach it --- its failure
is non-obscurity, a different one. The column is worth reading for what it does
\emph{not} say: four of the five shipping designs clear the bar this paper defines. The
claim was never that every deployed countermeasure is lethal. TGPO's mark is subject to
the bounded residual conceded in \S\ref{sec:residual-dr}. \textbf{Rewind}
trades individual custody for a remote delegate (\ref{r:remotewinner}). \textbf{Casa}'s collaborative
multisig --- the client's keys on separate hardware plus a Casa co-signing key ---
trades individual custody: the co-signer is a remote party, and a coercion-resistant
delegate is remote \emph{by definition}, so the cost is individual custody, not
non-geography (Casa asks the user to visit no site of its own)~\cite{casa}. \textbf{BitVault} trades individual custody
for the same reason Casa does --- a required co-signature on its primary path --- but
without obtaining what the surrender is meant to buy: it is the one row that pays a
property and, per \S\ref{sec:classify} above, still lands in the gray zone. Its
remaining two columns are left undetermined, for the reasons given there. The axis
tilts further to \ref{r:removerace} for a blunter reason (Remark~\ref{rem:r2-cost}):
the delegated rows are not self-custody at all --- their clearance assumes a
delegate that refuses even the legitimate owner --- and, the rescue capability being
\emph{transmissible}, they keep a clean-loss exposure the tacit route does not.
\emph{Not your keys, not your coins.}

\subsection{Empirical grounding in the registry}
\label{sub:empirical}

Two stipulations of the model lean on the physical-attack
registry~\cite{lopp-attacks} rather than on argument: the coercion-time budget
$t$ (\S\ref{sec:limits}) and the weighing of the murder cost $c$ against the
prize. The companion paper~\cite{denial-spiral} codes $352$ incidents from a
pinned snapshot and reports the method, the tables and the jurisdictional
breakdown; we take from it only what the stipulations require.

Long-hold modalities --- kidnap, abduction, prolonged captivity --- are a large
share of the coded set, which is what a budget $t$ measured in hours rather than
minutes assumes. Fatal outcomes are attested, so the terminal move
Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} predicts is not hypothetical. The fatality share is a
\emph{floor}: a robbery whose victim is killed is recorded as a homicide rather
than as a Bitcoin attack, so the cases most diagnostic of the Lemma are the least
likely to be counted at all. The long-hold share is bounded the other way --- a
media-sourced registry over-counts the dramatic, so the coded kidnap share
\emph{over}-states the long tail rather than establishing it.

That censoring is the reason this paper derives its bar rather than measuring
it. The exposure covariate --- what design a given victim was running --- has no
recorded answer anywhere, because obscurity does not advertise itself; and the
companion gives three further mechanisms that compound with it, the last of which
removes the response to the evidence rather than the evidence, and so survives
publication~\cite[\S6]{denial-spiral}. A field waiting for these data will keep
not finding what it is not positioned to see.
