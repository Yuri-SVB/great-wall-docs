\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\textbf{Coercion resistance.} The term is due to Juels, Catalano, and
Jakobsson~\cite{juels-coercion}, in electronic voting: a scheme is
coercion-resistant if a voter \emph{cannot prove} to a coercer how they voted, so
the coercer cannot verify compliance and the reason to coerce collapses. Our sense
is different and complementary. There the protected act is a \emph{choice} the
coercer wants to dictate and safety comes from \emph{unprovability}; here the
protected object is a \emph{bearer secret} the coercer wants to extract, and the
danger is not that compliance is unverifiable but that a released holder remains a
racer worth killing (Lemma~\ref{lem:drl}). We inherit the word, not the model.

\textbf{Secrets recognized but not articulated.} The tacit premise
(Assumption~\ref{ass:tkba}) --- a secret its holder can \emph{recognize} but cannot
\emph{transmit} --- has direct antecedents. Bojinov, Sanchez, Reber, Boneh, and
Lincoln~\cite{bojinov-rubberhose} train an authentication secret by \emph{implicit
learning} so that even a cooperating, coerced user cannot articulate it
(``rubber-hose'' resistance); this is the closest prior instantiation of our TKBA,
and TGPO can be read as carrying the same idea from an authentication token to a
self-custody key behind a memory-hard time-gate. The broader phenomenon --- identity
carried in a behavior that resists articulation --- is old: keystroke dynamics treat
idiosyncratic typing rhythm as a biometric~\cite{monrose-keystroke}, and WWII traffic
analysis identified individual wireless operators by the ``fist'' of their Morse
keying long before either was formalized~\cite{kahn-codebreakers}.

\textbf{Deniability versus unprovability.} Deniable
encryption~\cite{canetti-deniable} and the duress-PIN / panic-password schemes it
inspired let a coerced user \emph{plausibly lie} --- surrender a fake key, or open a
decoy. NNPP (Assumption~\ref{lem:nnpl}) is the sharp negative counterpart: rather
than proving a false positive (``this is the key''), the holder would need to prove a
true negative (``no other key exists''), which cannot be done. Deniability offers a
lie the coercer cannot disprove; we show the holder cannot furnish the assurance the
coercer would need, which is exactly why the coercer's only remaining lever is the
victim (\S\ref{sec:chain}). Note too (\S\ref{sub:fourprops}) that decoy and duress
schemes rest on the adversary's ignorance and so fail Kerckhoffs.

\textbf{Time-gates.} The construction's wall-clock gate is a time-lock puzzle in the
sense of Rivest, Shamir, and Wagner~\cite{rsw1996}; verifiable delay
functions~\cite{boneh-vdf} are the modern, publicly checkable form of the same
primitive, and either may instantiate the outsourced time-lock the orbit optionally
uses (\S\ref{sec:gw}).

\textbf{Kidnap economics and marginal deterrence.} The Lemma's shape is not new,
and it would be wrong to present it as though it were. Selten's kidnapping
game~\cite{selten-kidnapping} is the canonical formalization of the encounter we
model, and the criminological literature on \emph{marginal deterrence} ---
Stigler~\cite{stigler-enforcement}, formalized by
Shavell~\cite{shavell-marginal-deterrence} --- has long carried the observation
that an offender facing no incremental sanction has no reason to stop short of
the worse act, with \emph{witness elimination} as its standard illustration.
Read at that level of abstraction, the Deadly-Race Lemma is a witness-elimination
argument in which the thing the victim retains is not testimony but a claim on a
rivalrous prize. Independent arrival at the same mechanism from an unrelated
field corroborates it; we claim no priority over either literature.

What is worth stating precisely is \emph{where} our setting departs from
Selten's, because the departure is the reason our conclusion differs from his.
Selten assumes the kidnapper's threat to execute is credible \emph{even though
executing cannot improve his position} --- and in classic kidnap-for-ransom that
assumption is correct, because the prize is a ransom transferred by a third
party and is not contestable once paid. The hostage's survival threatens nothing
the kidnapper holds. Bitcoin self-custody removes exactly that property. The
prize is rivalrous and bearer, and a design that leaves $\Victim$ a
feasible path leaves it \emph{contestable after release}: a surviving
$\Victim$ may spend first and take $W$. In Selten's terms our
$(1-p_A)$ is identically zero; the Deadly Race is his game with a non-degenerate
post-release contest, and it is that single structural change that turns
execution from a threat instrument into an action with positive expected value.
The contribution here is therefore not the mechanism but its locus: marginal
deterrence studies how a \emph{legislator's} penalty schedule moves this margin,
whereas we show that a \emph{custody designer} moves the same margin, and that a
widely shipped class of designs moves it the wrong way.

Two qualifications follow. First, the counterweight is real and we do not
minimize it: Iqbal, Masson and Abbott~\cite{iqbal-selten-extension} extend
Selten precisely by letting the capture probability rise when the hostage is
executed, which is our $c$ made explicit. Our claim is that the gain side can
exceed $c$ for large $W$, not that $c$ is small --- and Lemma~\ref{lem:drl} is
stated as a threshold condition for exactly that reason. Second, the deterrence
literature in this vein --- Lapan and Sandler on precommitment to
non-negotiation~\cite{lapan-sandler-bargain} --- addresses whether the encounter
\emph{occurs}. Our results concern the game once it has, and we are careful to
keep the two apart wherever a design claims deterrence as a structural property
(\S\ref{sec:classify}).
